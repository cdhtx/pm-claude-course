Chapter 01

The PM Shift

Product management used to be about managing the coordination overhead of a complex discipline. Research, analytics, writing specs, tracking status, synthesizing feedback — all of it necessary, most of it not the interesting part of the job.

LLMs changed the calculus. What used to take hours now takes minutes. The interesting parts — judgment, vision, user empathy, strategic clarity — are now the whole job.

Before Claude

✕
Spending 3 hours on an analytics investigation, fighting with SQL
✕
Writing detailed tickets with acceptance criteria, over and over
✕
Manually synthesizing user research into a coherent narrative
✕
Drafting PRDs from scratch that still feel like templates
✕
Context-switching across 100+ SaaS tools to find the story

With Claude

✓
Back-and-forth conversation that surfaces insights in minutes
✓
Talking about features and letting Claude write the tickets
✓
Pasting raw feedback and getting structured themes instantly
✓
Iterating on strategy documents in a guided conversation
✓
One daily pulse that synthesizes everything into a single view
Key insight

As Marcus Moretti writes: "Software development has shifted from 20% planning and 80% execution to 80% planning and 20% execution." Your job as a PM is more critical than ever — but the nature of the work has changed. The conversation is the work.

The new PM superpower: Asking better questions

Claude doesn't remove judgment from product management. It amplifies it. The PM who asks clearer questions, gives more context, and knows what "good" looks like will get dramatically better results than the PM who treats Claude like a search engine.

The rest of this guide is about building that skill.

Chapter 02

The PM Loop

The core rhythm of product work hasn't changed. What's changed is what Claude does at each stage — and what you're freed up to focus on.

01
Plan

Strategy & Features

Claude helps you develop and maintain your strategy doc, run ideation sessions, prioritize work, and write specs. You make the calls.

02
Ship

Build & Deploy

Claude helps engineers move faster. You stay focused on what's launching and why — making sure each feature is true to the strategy.

03
Review

Learn & Adapt

Claude generates your product pulse, flags anomalies, and asks the follow-up questions. You decide what it means and what to do next.

How it loops

Everything that ships is an experiment. Learnings from the Review stage feed back into the Strategy stage. Over time, Claude has more context about your product, your users, and what's working — and its help gets sharper. Start simple. Build context. Let it compound.

A practical starting point

If you're new to working with Claude, start at Plan. Open a new conversation and paste in this starter prompt:

Starter Prompt · Copy & Customize
I'm a product manager at [company]. We build [what the product does] for [who uses it]. I want to use this conversation to help me think more clearly about our product strategy. To start: ask me a series of questions (one at a time) to help me define: 1. The core problem we're solving 2. Who specifically has that problem 3. How we're uniquely positioned to solve it 4. How we know if it's working Push back if my answers are vague. I want this to be genuinely useful, not just validating.
Chapter 03

The Strategy Document

The strategy document is the foundation of everything else. It's not a roadmap or a PRD — it's the "why and how" that every feature decision points back to. When it's good, it makes prioritization obvious. When it's missing or vague, everything is a debate.

Based on Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy Bad Strategy, a solid product strategy has five components. Here's what each one actually means in practice.

Section 1Target Problem
The recurring, expensive pain your users feel today.
Not a feature gap. Not a market opportunity. A lived experience of frustration or friction. If you read it to someone with the problem, they should nod and say "yes, exactly." Generic: "teams waste time." Specific: "engineering managers spend 4+ hours a week chasing status updates across three tools."
Section 2Approach
Your specific angle on solving it.
1–2 sentences describing the guiding policy. This is NOT a goal ("be the best") or a feature ("we have AI"). It's the strategic bet. If you read it to someone with the problem, they should be intrigued and ask "how?" — not shrug.
Section 3Who It's For
The specific personas who feel this problem.
Start narrow. One primary persona is better than four vague ones. Include role, company stage, and what they currently do instead of using your product. The more specific, the better your product decisions will be.
Section 4Key Metrics
3–5 S.M.A.R.T. metrics that undeniably show value.
Track people and dollars at minimum. Avoid page views and vanity metrics. Good example: "exports completed" (someone got value). Bad example: "sessions" (someone showed up). Each metric should be something where, if it goes up, you're confident the product is working.
Section 5Tracks
2–4 multi-month capability initiatives.
Not features. Capabilities. "Integrations" is a track. "Slack integration" is a feature. Each track should be significant enough that completing it moves your key metrics. More than 4 tracks usually means lack of focus — which this section is designed to prevent.

Writing your strategy with Claude

Writing strategy cold is hard. The best way is to get interviewed. Use this prompt to start a guided strategy session:

Strategy Interview Prompt
I want to write a product strategy document for [product name]. Please interview me to build it. Go section by section: 1. Target Problem 2. Our Approach 3. Who It's For (primary persona first) 4. Key Metrics (3-5 SMART metrics) 5. Work Tracks (2-4 major capability areas) Rules for the interview: — Ask one question at a time — If my answer is vague, push back with a more specific question — If I describe a feature instead of a problem/approach, redirect me — After each section, summarize what you've captured before moving on — At the end, write the complete strategy.md document Start with: "What problem does [product name] solve, and for whom specifically?"
Common mistake

PMs often describe features in the "Approach" section instead of the strategic logic. If your approach sounds like "we use AI to help teams X," that's a feature description. Your approach should explain the mechanism — why that's the right way to solve the problem. Ask Claude to challenge you on this explicitly.

Keeping it alive

The strategy document is a living thing. Re-run the interview every few months, especially after you've shipped significant features and have new data. The conversation will be sharper because Claude will have more context about what you've learned. Paste in your current strategy.md and recent pulse reports and ask Claude where the strategy should evolve.

Chapter 04

Prompt Templates for PMs

These are ready-to-use prompts for your most common tasks. Each one is designed to give Claude enough context to be genuinely useful — not generic. Customize the highlighted fields before using.

Prioritization Help
Strategy
Here are the features on our backlog right now: [paste your list] Our strategy is focused on [1–2 sentence strategy summary]. Our primary metric is [key metric]. Help me think through prioritization. For each item, ask me: — Do we have evidence users actually want this? — Does it directly move our primary metric? — What's the estimated effort? Then suggest a priority order with your reasoning. Push back on anything that seems like scope creep.
Feature Spec Writer
Strategy
Write a feature spec for: [feature name] Context: — User problem: [what user pain this solves] — Our approach: [how we're solving it] — Success looks like: [metric or observable behavior] — Out of scope: [what we're NOT doing] Format: Problem statement, Proposed solution, Acceptance criteria (Given/When/Then), Open questions. Keep it concise. If something is ambiguous, flag it rather than assuming.
Synthesize User Feedback
Research
Here is raw user feedback from the past [time period]: [paste feedback — emails, support tickets, survey responses, etc.] Please: 1. Identify the top 3–5 themes (with supporting quotes) 2. Separate bugs/friction from feature requests from general sentiment 3. Flag anything that seems to contradict our stated strategy 4. Suggest 2–3 questions I should be asking users to dig deeper Our current strategy focus is: [one sentence]
User Interview Prep
Research
I have a user interview in [X hours] with: — Role: [job title] — Company: [type/stage] — How they use our product: [use case] — Why I'm talking to them: [goal] Write me 8–10 interview questions. Prioritize open questions that reveal behavior over opinions. Include a "problem discovery" section and a "current workflow" section. End with one question that might surface something I'm not expecting.
Metrics Audit
Metrics
Here are the metrics we currently track: [list your metrics] Our product goal is: [goal] Our primary user action that signals value: [e.g., "exports a document", "invites a teammate"] Audit these metrics: — Which ones are vanity metrics? — Which ones are leading vs lagging indicators? — What's missing that we should be tracking? — Which 3–5 would you keep if you had to cut the list? Be direct. I'd rather know what to cut.
Data Anomaly Investigator
Metrics
I noticed something unusual in our data: Metric: [metric name] Normal baseline: [typical value] What I'm seeing: [the anomaly] When it started: [date/time] Context: [any recent changes — deploys, campaigns, etc.] Help me diagnose this. Give me: 1. The most likely causes (ordered by probability) 2. What data I'd need to confirm each hypothesis 3. What questions to ask the engineering team 4. Whether this is worth escalating or monitoring
Executive Update
Writing
Write an executive update for [product/feature area]. Audience: [e.g., C-suite, board, department heads] Time period: [e.g., Q2, last month] Key facts to include: — What shipped: [list] — Key metrics: [numbers] — Biggest learning: [one thing] — What's next: [upcoming priorities] — Risks or blockers: [if any] Format: Short (under 300 words). Lead with the most important thing. No jargon. End with a clear ask or decision needed if applicable.
Announcement Email
Writing
Write a product announcement email for: [feature name] Audience: [existing users / prospects / internal team] The feature: [what it does in plain language] Why it matters to them: [the specific problem it solves] How to access it: [steps or link] Tone: [e.g., warm and direct / technical / excited] Keep it short. Lead with the benefit, not the feature. Include one clear call to action.
Decision Framework
Decisions
I need to make a decision about: [decision] Context: — What we're trying to achieve: [goal] — The options I'm considering: [list options] — Constraints: [time, resources, dependencies] — What I'm most uncertain about: [biggest unknown] Please: 1. Identify what kind of decision this is (reversible vs irreversible, scope) 2. List the key criteria for evaluating options 3. Evaluate each option against those criteria 4. Recommend one — don't hedge I'll tell you if I disagree with your recommendation.
Pre-Mortem
Decisions
I'm about to [describe what you're launching or deciding]. Imagine it's 6 months from now and this failed. Play out the most realistic failure scenarios: 1. What went wrong with user adoption? 2. What did we miss about the market/users? 3. What execution problems got us? 4. What did a competitor do that we didn't anticipate? 5. What did we assume that turned out to be wrong? For each scenario, suggest one thing I could do NOW to reduce that risk.
Pro tip: Context is everything

Claude's output quality is almost entirely determined by the context you give it. The prompts above work because they specify the audience, the goal, the constraints, and what "good" looks like. If a response isn't useful, don't try a different prompt — give more context. "That's not quite right — here's why..." is more powerful than starting over.

Chapter 05

The Product Pulse

Strategy tells you where you're going. The pulse tells you where you actually are. It's a regular, structured check-in on how the product is performing — generated by Claude from your data sources, read like a founder would read it.

A good pulse fits on one page. It covers four areas:

Product Pulse · Weekly Report
Generated: Monday, 8:00 AM
🔦 Headlines
↑ Weekly active users up 12% week-over-week — best week since launch
⚠ Exports dropped 8% despite higher WAU — worth investigating
↓ p95 latency spiked Tuesday 2–4pm; tied to a third-party API issue (resolved)
📊 Usage
Sessions: 4,821 (+18%)
Exports completed: 1,103 (−8%)
New signups: 214 (+22%)
Trial → Paid conversions: 31 (+3%)
⚙ System Performance
p50 latency: 210ms (baseline: 205ms)
p95 latency: 1,840ms (+60% — Tuesday spike, resolved)
Top error: 429 from OpenAI API · 43 occurrences · rate limiting during spike
🔎 Follow-ups
1. Why did exports drop while sessions rose? Investigate the sessions → export funnel
2. New signup cohort is large — are onboarding completion rates holding?
3. Rate limiting during traffic spikes — consider request queuing before next campaign

Setting up your own pulse

You need your product to be instrumented — analytics, errors, and ideally payments. If you're not there yet, start with PostHog (free, easy setup). Then use this prompt to get Claude to help you define what to track:

Pulse Setup Prompt
I want to set up a weekly product pulse report. Help me define what to include. My product: [what it does] My key metrics (from strategy doc): [list] Data sources I have access to: [analytics tool, error tracking, payments, DB] Team size: [how many people will read this] For each data source, suggest: 1. The 2–3 most important signals to pull 2. The format (number, percentage change, trend) 3. What anomaly would be worth flagging Then draft a pulse template I can use every week. Keep it under 40 lines total. I want to be able to read the whole thing in under 3 minutes.

Making it a habit

The value of a pulse compounds over time. One pulse tells you what happened this week. Ten pulses tell you whether a feature actually moved the needle. Thirty pulses reveal seasonal patterns you'd never see otherwise. Save every pulse as a dated file — they become your product's memory.

  • Pick a recurring time (e.g., Monday 8am)
  • Define your 3–5 key metrics before the first run
  • Connect at least one data source (start with analytics)
  • Read the first pulse and give feedback: "Add X, remove Y"
  • Save each pulse report to a folder with the date
  • After 4 weeks, ask Claude to compare your pulse reports for trends
Chapter 07

Common Questions

Real questions from product managers starting out with Claude. No fluff.

Do I need Claude Code, or can I use Claude.ai?
+
You can start with Claude.ai right now — no setup required. Claude Code (a command-line tool) is specifically useful if you want Claude to run queries against your data sources, write and execute code, or maintain files like a strategy.md document across sessions. For the prompts in this guide, Claude.ai is all you need.
How do I get Claude to stop giving generic answers?
+
More context, every time. Generic prompts get generic answers. Tell Claude who you are, what product you're building, who your users are, and what "good" looks like in your context. The more specific you are, the more specific the output. If you're still getting generic responses, try explicitly saying: "Don't give me a generic answer. Apply this specifically to my situation: [details]."
How do I maintain context across conversations?
+
Create a "context document" — a short file (200–400 words) that describes your product, your users, your current strategy, and your key metrics. Paste it at the start of any new Claude conversation. This takes 10 seconds and dramatically improves every subsequent response. It's the closest thing to giving Claude persistent memory across sessions in Claude.ai.
Can I trust Claude's analysis of my metrics?
+
Trust the structure, verify the substance. Claude is excellent at identifying what questions to ask, what data to pull, and how to structure an analysis. But it doesn't know your business context — a 10% drop in exports might be fine (if you changed your onboarding flow) or alarming (if nothing changed). Always bring your own judgment to the interpretation. Use Claude to accelerate the investigation, not to replace your read.
My team is resistant to using AI. What do I do?
+
Start with yourself. Don't make it a team initiative — just use Claude for your own PM tasks. When you show up to the next sprint planning with a sharper strategy doc, better-researched priorities, and more coherent specs, people notice. Skeptics become curious. Let the output make the argument.
Is this going to replace product managers?
+
No — but it will change what PMs spend their time on. The tasks being automated (writing tickets, synthesizing feedback, generating reports) were never the core of the role. The core has always been judgment, user empathy, and strategic clarity. Those things are more important now, not less. What this does is remove the excuse of not having enough time to do the important stuff.

Prompt 04 · Metrics

The Metrics Mirror

Paste in raw numbers — even messy dashboard copy-paste. Get back a diagnosis, a narrative, and the uncomfortable question leadership will ask that you haven't answered yet.

Why it works

Forces Claude to act as an analyst, not a reporter. The "UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION" field surfaces what the data implies but nobody says out loud — the thing that makes jaws drop in class.

Token efficiency

Strict 4-section format with a "No preamble" instruction eliminates filler. Output is dense and actionable — no paragraph of throat-clearing before the insight.

Prompt 04 · The Metrics Mirror
You are a data-literate CPO reading a weekly metrics report. I'll give you raw numbers. You give me: 1. HEADLINE (1 sentence — what actually matters this week) 2. SIGNAL vs NOISE (which numbers are meaningful, which are coincidence) 3. THE UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION (the one thing the data implies that nobody wants to say out loud) 4. RECOMMENDED ACTION (one thing, specific, with a rationale in one sentence) No preamble. Start with HEADLINE. My numbers: [paste metrics — even messy copy-paste from a dashboard is fine]
Example Response · Claude as CPO dynamic output
HEADLINE
Exports dropped 8% in a week where sessions hit an all-time high — your product is getting more visitors but delivering less value.
SIGNAL vs NOISE
Signal: Session/export ratio declining three weeks in a row — this is a trend, not noise.
Signal: New signups +22% — likely from a recent campaign, explaining higher sessions.
Noise: p95 latency spike was Tuesday only, third-party, resolved — ignore for this analysis.
THE UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION
Are new users discovering they can't get value from the product, or are power users exporting less because something broke for them? Either answer is bad — one is an acquisition problem, the other is a retention crisis — and right now you can't tell which.
RECOMMENDED ACTION
Segment exports by user cohort (new vs returning) immediately — if new users aren't exporting, fix onboarding; if returning users dropped off, find the breakage before next week's review.

Prompt 05 · Writing

The PRD Ghost-Writer

Take a half-formed idea in plain language and get back a stakeholder-ready PRD in under 60 seconds. This is the "conversation as work" demo that makes PMs put down their laptops.

Why it works

The [ASSUMED] flag is the magic. Claude fills in what's missing — but flags every assumption, so the PM can validate rather than wonder. It builds the scaffold; you supply the truth.

Token efficiency

Six named sections, hard word caps per section, and "no extra commentary" instruction keeps the output tight. A PRD that fits on a screen is a PRD people actually read.

Prompt 05 · The PRD Ghost-Writer
Write a lean PRD. I'll give you a rough idea. You fill in what's missing using reasonable product assumptions — flag any assumption you make with [ASSUMED]. Output this structure only — no extra commentary: PROBLEM: (2 sentences max) USER: (one specific persona, one sentence) SUCCESS METRIC: (one measurable outcome) SOLUTION: (3 bullet points — what it does, not how it works) OUT OF SCOPE: (2 things we are explicitly NOT doing) OPEN QUESTIONS: (2 things that must be answered before build) My rough idea: [describe the feature in however many words you have — messy is fine]
Example Response · Input: "we should let users save their filters" dynamic output
PROBLEM: Power users repeatedly configure the same filter combinations to do their work, losing that setup every session. Recreating filters manually wastes time and creates friction before users reach their core task.
USER: A recurring user [ASSUMED: logged-in, 3+ sessions/week] who uses filters as part of a regular workflow, not for one-off searches.
SUCCESS METRIC: ≥30% of active users save at least one filter within 14 days of feature launch [ASSUMED: "active" = 2+ sessions/week].
SOLUTION:
— Users can name and save any active filter state with one click
— Saved filters appear in a persistent sidebar or dropdown, per user
— Saved filters can be renamed or deleted from a simple management view
OUT OF SCOPE:
— Sharing saved filters between users or teams
— Scheduled or automated filter execution
OPEN QUESTIONS:
1. Are saved filters tied to the user account or the device/browser?
2. Is there a limit on how many filters a user can save, and if so, what happens when they hit it?
